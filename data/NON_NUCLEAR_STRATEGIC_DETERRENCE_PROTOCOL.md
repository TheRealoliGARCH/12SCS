# Non-Nuclear Strategic Deterrence Recognition Protocol

## Purpose

This protocol completes Dimension 1, **Nuclear and Strategic Deterrence**, for States that do not possess a publicly recognized nuclear arsenal.

The objective is to recognize demonstrated strategic deterrence without equating deterrence with possession of nuclear weapons.

## Core principle

For a non-nuclear State,

$$
N_i^{*}=f(A_i,C_i,E_i,T_i,G_i,V_i,I_i),
$$

where:

- $A_i$: alliance or security-partnership support that is actually available to the State;
- $C_i$: conventional denial and defence capability;
- $E_i$: economic resilience and capacity to impose or absorb strategic costs;
- $T_i$: strategic technology and information capability;
- $G_i$: geographic and territorial characteristics affecting denial and coercion;
- $V_i$: civil-defence and continuity-of-government resilience;
- $I_i$: institutional credibility and demonstrated resolve.

These components are measured from public evidence and are not simply imported from the other eleven dimensions. Cross-dimension correlations are retained for later analysis rather than counted twice in the recognition decision.

## Nuclear component

No State receives a nuclear-strategic score from speculation about undeclared weapons, covert programmes, hypothetical weaponization, or inferred technical latency.

A State's peaceful nuclear-science or nuclear-energy capability may be recorded as contextual evidence where publicly documented, but it does not by itself constitute nuclear deterrence. No technical pathway for weapon development is inferred or constructed.

Accordingly, the protocol recognizes **strategic deterrence without requiring nuclear possession**, while preserving a strict evidentiary boundary around nuclear weapons capability.

## Alliance and extended deterrence

An alliance contributes to recognition only to the extent that the State is formally covered by a credible security commitment or demonstrably integrated security arrangement. A generic diplomatic relationship is insufficient.

For NATO members, collective defence is treated as an observable strategic input. NATO describes deterrence as an integrated mix of nuclear, conventional and missile-defence capabilities complemented by space and cyber capabilities. The contribution of an individual non-nuclear Ally is therefore recognized as part of an alliance-level deterrence architecture, not as ownership of the Alliance's nuclear arsenal.

## Neutrality

Neutrality is not automatically assigned a positive or negative value. For a neutral State, deterrence can arise from credible armed neutrality, territorial denial, resilience, geography, institutional credibility, and the expectation that neutrality will be respected.

Swiss armed neutrality is therefore evaluated as a distinctive strategic architecture. Cooperation with NATO through the Partnership for Peace is treated as cooperation, not collective-defence membership.

## Recognition levels

- **Level 0:** no sufficiently demonstrated strategic-deterrence capability under this protocol.
- **Level 1:** limited strategic deterrence, principally local or highly constrained.
- **Level 2:** substantial national or regional strategic deterrence without an independent nuclear arsenal.
- **Level 3:** major strategic deterrence with significant regional or wider systemic consequences.
- **Level 4:** systemic strategic deterrence with consequences extending materially beyond the State's immediate region.

## Current provisional applications

### Switzerland

Switzerland is recognized at **Level 2**. Evidence includes permanent armed neutrality, an independent armed force capable of territorial defence, established civil-protection and continuity institutions, and structured security cooperation with NATO while remaining outside NATO collective defence.

The score does not treat Switzerland as a nuclear-armed State and does not infer a latent weapons programme.

### Belgium

Belgium is recognized at **Level 2**. Its strategic deterrence is principally alliance-based: Belgium is a NATO Ally and therefore participates in the Alliance's collective deterrence and defence architecture. Belgium's contribution is recognized as an alliance-enabled capability, not as an independent Belgian nuclear arsenal.

### Taiwan

Taiwan is recognized at **Level 3**. Publicly documented evidence describes a multi-domain denial and resilient-defence strategy, substantial conventional capability, geographic advantages for homeland defence, strategic communications, cyber and information capabilities, civil-military integration, reserve-force preparation, and whole-of-society resilience exercises.

No nuclear weapons capability is inferred from Taiwan's technological or peaceful nuclear history.

## Evidence rule

The recognition score is a measurement of demonstrated strategic capability, not a judgment of political legitimacy, moral worth, military intent, or desirability of conflict.

The protocol is deliberately compatible with later convergence analysis: a State can have high strategic deterrence while remaining non-nuclear, and two States can obtain the same recognition level through fundamentally different capability portfolios.
